Damp and mould in rented homes is not a cosmetic nuisance. It is a serious health hazard that causes respiratory infections, worsens asthma, and in the most tragic case on record, contributed to the death of two-year-old Awaab Ishak in Rochdale in 2020. His death prompted a national reckoning with how damp and mould complaints are handled in rented housing, leading to significant legal changes that strengthen renter protections.

This guide covers what those legal changes mean for you as a renter, how to identify and report damp and mould effectively, what your landlord is and is not responsible for, and what to do when complaints are ignored.

## The Legal Framework: What Has Changed

### Awaab's Law

Awaab's Law, introduced as an amendment to the Social Housing (Regulation) Act 2023, sets strict timelines for social housing landlords to address damp and mould:

- **14 calendar days** to investigate a reported hazard after being notified
- **7 calendar days** to begin repair works after investigation
- **Emergency hazards** (those posing imminent risk to health) must be made safe within 24 hours

These timelines apply to registered providers of social housing (housing associations and council housing). The government has indicated its intention to extend similar requirements to the private rented sector, though the timeline for this extension remains under discussion as of 2026.

### The Renters' Rights Act 2025

The Renters' Rights Act 2025 makes several changes that directly affect damp and mould disputes:

- **Abolishes Section 21 no-fault evictions.** This is the single most important change for renters reporting disrepair. Previously, many renters avoided reporting damp and mould because they feared retaliatory eviction under Section 21. That route is now closed.
- **Strengthens the Decent Homes Standard** for the private rented sector, bringing it in line with social housing requirements.
- **Gives tenants the right to challenge substandard conditions** without fear of losing their home.

### Homes (Fitness for Human Habitation) Act 2018

This act, already in force, requires that rented properties are fit for human habitation at the start of and throughout the tenancy. Significant damp and mould can render a property unfit. Tenants can take their landlord to court if the property fails this standard, and the court can order repairs and award compensation.

### Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, Section 11

Section 11 places a statutory obligation on landlords to keep the structure and exterior of the property in repair, and to keep installations for water, gas, electricity, sanitation, and heating in proper working order. Structural causes of damp (leaking roofs, defective guttering, rising damp, failed damp-proof courses) fall squarely within this obligation.

## Types of Damp: Identifying What You Have

Understanding what type of damp you are dealing with helps determine responsibility and the correct fix.

### Condensation Damp

**What it looks like:** Water droplets on windows, black mould on window frames and sills, mould in corners of rooms (especially behind furniture against exterior walls), mould on bathroom ceilings.

**What causes it:** Warm, moisture-laden air meeting cold surfaces. Common in poorly ventilated properties, especially during winter. Cooking, showering, and drying laundry indoors all contribute.

**Who is responsible:** This is the most contested type. Landlords often blame tenant lifestyle, and in some cases they are partly right. However, if the property lacks adequate ventilation (broken or absent extractor fans, sealed-shut windows, no trickle vents), the structural inadequacy is the landlord's responsibility. The presence of adequate ventilation is key to determining responsibility.

### Rising Damp

**What it looks like:** A tide mark of damp and staining on ground-floor walls, typically up to about one metre high. Peeling wallpaper, salt deposits (white crystalline residue), and a musty smell at skirting-board level.

**What causes it:** Moisture from the ground rising through the walls due to a failed or absent damp-proof course (DPC).

**Who is responsible:** Always the landlord. This is a structural issue.

### Penetrating Damp

**What it looks like:** Damp patches on walls or ceilings, often corresponding to external defects. May worsen during or after rainfall. Can appear at any level of the building.

**What causes it:** Water entering through the building envelope: leaking roofs, cracked render, defective pointing, blocked or broken guttering, failed window seals.

**Who is responsible:** Always the landlord. This is a structural issue.

## How to Report Damp and Mould: Step by Step

### Step 1: Document Everything

Before contacting your landlord, gather evidence:

- **Photographs** of all affected areas, with dates. Include close-ups of mould growth and wider shots showing the location in the room.
- **A written description** of where the damp or mould is, how long it has been present, and whether it is getting worse.
- **Health impact notes.** If you or anyone in your household is experiencing symptoms (coughing, wheezing, skin irritation, respiratory infections), note these. If you have visited a GP, request a letter linking symptoms to housing conditions.
- **Evidence of your own mitigation efforts.** If you ventilate regularly, use extractor fans, and heat the property adequately, note this. It counters the "tenant lifestyle" defence.

### Step 2: Send a Written Report to Your Landlord

Contact your landlord or letting agent **in writing**. Email is ideal because it creates an automatic timestamp. If you send a letter, use recorded delivery.

Your report should include:
- Your name and the property address
- A clear description of the problem and its location
- How long the problem has existed
- Photographs (attached or enclosed)
- Any health symptoms experienced
- A request for inspection and repair within a reasonable timeframe (14 days is standard for non-emergency issues)

**Keep copies of everything you send.**

### Step 3: Follow Up

If you receive no response within 14 days, send a follow-up email referencing your original report and the date it was sent. State that you will contact your local council's environmental health team if the issue is not addressed.

### Step 4: Contact the Council

If your landlord continues to ignore the problem, contact your local council's environmental health department. They have the power to:

- Inspect the property
- Serve an improvement notice requiring the landlord to carry out repairs
- Issue a prohibition order (in severe cases, preventing the property from being occupied)
- Prosecute landlords who fail to comply with notices
- Issue civil penalties of up to £30,000 per offence

To find your council's environmental health team, search "[your council name] environmental health housing" online, or call your council's main switchboard.

### Step 5: Seek Legal Advice if Needed

If the situation remains unresolved, you have several options:

- **Shelter** (shelter.org.uk) provides free housing advice and can help with disrepair claims
- **Citizens Advice** (citizensadvice.org.uk) offers free legal guidance
- **Legal aid** may be available for housing disrepair cases depending on your income
- **The Housing Ombudsman** handles complaints against social housing providers
- You can take your landlord to court under the Homes (Fitness for Human Habitation) Act 2018

## What You Can Do While Waiting for Repairs

These measures reduce mould growth and improve air quality while you wait for your landlord to act. They do not replace structural repairs.

- **Ventilate daily.** Open windows for 10 to 15 minutes, even in winter. Use extractor fans in the kitchen and bathroom during and after cooking and showering.
- **Heat the property consistently.** Maintaining a steady temperature (at least 18°C in living areas) reduces condensation. Intermittent heating creates temperature swings that worsen condensation.
- **Clean existing mould** with a solution of one part white vinegar to one part water, or a fungicidal wash from a DIY store. Wear a mask and gloves. Do not use bleach on porous surfaces — it does not kill mould roots.
- **Move furniture away from exterior walls** by at least 10 centimetres to allow air circulation.
- **Avoid drying laundry on radiators** without opening a window. If you must dry indoors, use a well-ventilated area.
- **Use a dehumidifier** if you can afford one. A 10 to 20 litre unit costs £100 to £250 and makes a significant difference in damp flats.

## Common Landlord Responses and How to Counter Them

### "It's a lifestyle issue — you need to ventilate more."

This is sometimes partially true, but it is overused as a deflection. Counter it by documenting your ventilation habits and pointing out any structural deficiencies: broken extractor fans, sealed windows, lack of trickle vents, single-glazed windows, or inadequate insulation. If the property makes adequate ventilation impossible or impractical, the cause is structural.

### "Just open the windows."

Opening windows in winter to prevent mould while paying to heat the property is not a reasonable solution to a structural ventilation problem. If the property requires windows to be open in sub-zero temperatures to prevent mould, the ventilation design is inadequate.

### "We'll send someone to paint over it."

Painting over mould without addressing the underlying cause is not a repair. The mould will return. Insist on investigation and treatment of the root cause.

### "You should buy a dehumidifier."

A dehumidifier is a reasonable interim measure, but the cost of purchasing and running one (electricity) should not fall on the tenant when the cause is structural. Some tenants have successfully claimed the cost of a dehumidifier as part of a disrepair claim.

## When Damp Is a Medical Emergency

Seek medical attention if anyone in the household, particularly children, elderly residents, or people with existing respiratory conditions, experiences:

- Persistent coughing or wheezing that worsens at home
- Recurring chest infections
- Skin rashes or irritation
- Worsening asthma symptoms
- Any allergic reaction that improves when away from the property

Ask your GP for a letter stating that housing conditions are contributing to or worsening the symptoms. This letter is powerful evidence in a disrepair claim.
